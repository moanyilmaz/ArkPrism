\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Evidence Supports}
The evaluation assigns each claim to an oracle with the matching unit. ArkSourceFirst60 supports exact-location occurrence precision and recall on an output-independent, size/import-stratified real-project sample that includes 44 confirmed zero projects. ArkIdentityBench isolates recognition mechanisms under matched positive and negative patterns. HapBench and ArkAsyncBench support case-level flow classification and asynchronous mechanism attribution, respectively. The 120-project stress audit tests dense reported identities, while the 1,014-project corpus supports prevalence, robustness, and scalability observations rather than ground-truth accuracy. Within the corpus, detector-local sinks, typed privacy-data paths, and framework-input paths remain separate evidence universes; a conservative endpoint relation links detector usages only to compatible privacy-data sources.

The resulting subgraphs should also be interpreted as audit evidence. A may-flow to a log or UI operation is not automatically a privacy violation, and a local fallback chain is not a complete lifecycle path. Provenance labels expose whether an edge came from IFDS, callback supplementation, lifecycle modeling, or report-CG recovery, but they do not assign a probability that the edge is feasible. The final-run audit validates schema integrity, endpoint resolution, provenance presence, and link indices; it is not a user study or a semantic proof of every subgraph edge.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\textbf{Benchmark validity.} HapBench is the only public, executable OpenHarmony taint benchmark available to us. Its 67 cases provide controlled positive and negative examples, but remain small and often synthetic. We reconstruct its case oracle from the artifact's inline \texttt{leak}/\texttt{no leak} annotations and cross-check category totals against the HapFlow paper. The outputs produced by the released executable artifact do not exactly reproduce the paper's aggregate table. We therefore publish every case label and actual output, and retain the source annotations rather than modifying the oracle to match a reported aggregate.

\textbf{Source-benchmark validity.} ArkSourceFirst60 is frozen from project scale and configured-import strata without reading detector reports. Candidate generation is a navigation aid: every accepted and rejected item is decided from source context, and exact file hashes and locations make those decisions auditable. This design directly includes API-positive and confirmed-zero projects, but its 60 projects and author-reviewed labels delimit the population to which the observed 90/90 result applies. The separate Top-120 audit is intentionally output-ranked and therefore contributes high-volume precision evidence. Together, the two sets expose different failure modes rather than allowing one selection strategy to stand for both accuracy and stress testing.

\textbf{Corpus validity.} The corpus contains applications, libraries, and many capability demonstrations. Of 1,015 candidate directories, 1,014 satisfy the complete-analysis criterion; one resource-bound partial result is excluded and retained in the artifact. Duplicate templates could inflate prevalence, so we fingerprint source token streams and repeat project-level measurements after retaining one representative per exact- and near-clone cluster. The small change in detection rates under both sensitivity analyses reduces, but does not eliminate, concerns about representativeness. Marketplace and industrial applications remain necessary for broader external validity.

\textbf{Configuration validity.} Source/sink definitions, package migration rules, SDK version, call-graph mode, and resource bounds all influence static-taint results. Our tool comparison fixes the HapBench cases, API-17 SDK, and query, reports complete versus incomplete executions, and uses the author-released HapFlow artifact. We intentionally omit the earlier API-20-rule/API-17-loader coverage percentage because it confounded SDK availability, overload metadata, and schema capability. The 716-entry detector rule set is deduplicated and hashed; the release also records 67 explicit category corrections. Separately, the IFDS loader accepts 257 typed privacy-data rules and two explicit framework inputs after removing 655 untyped detector-to-source promotions and resolving a further set of unknown, stale, or externally scoped carriers. An executable SDK audit confirms that each retained privacy-data rule resolves to one API-20 declaration with a matching carrier. These checks eliminate concrete classes of invalid seeds, overload ambiguity, and category pollution, but they still do not prove semantic completeness: the artifact does not establish that all SDK privacy APIs are covered or that every retained category and carrier matches an independent expert oracle. Android analyzers are not executed because they consume Dalvik/Android inputs and framework models; their published numbers would not answer the same ArkTS query.

\textbf{Asynchronous-analysis validity.} HapBench isolates the core IR/dispatch/lifecycle mechanisms, while ArkAsyncBench supplies a balanced source-first oracle for T1/T4/T5 carrier recovery. Its provenance decomposition shows dual core/supplement support for T1 and T5 and a nine-case supplement-exclusive T4 increment. The 26-project diagnostic confirms eight corresponding real-code paths spanning all three constructs. The analysis contract covers these typed callback, Promise-\texttt{then}, and \texttt{await} patterns; arbitrary Promise combinators and framework-defined callback lifetimes remain outside this experiment's oracle.

\subsection{Limitations}
\mytool is static and source-based. Its contract excludes runtime-loaded code, native C/C++ bridges, server-side policy, and user-consent state; cross-language analysis such as HarmoBridge~\cite{harmobridge} is complementary. Receiver-origin recovery is bounded, and the asynchronous supplement targets the ArkTS continuation patterns formalized in Section~\ref{subsec:async_binding} rather than arbitrary event-loop behavior. The reported subgraphs are therefore may-analysis evidence: API presence and a source-to-sink path support code review but do not by themselves establish a legal or policy violation.
